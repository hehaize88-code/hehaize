# One CNShopper order arrived in part: working out which units are still missing
A warehouse picture showing one product does not automatically mean the seller sent only one. Other pieces might be in a second domestic parcel, inside the same carton, listed under a different warehouse entry or absent from the shipment. Establish the expected contents and the evidence for each physical delivery before deciding which question belongs with the seller and which belongs with the warehouse.

## Start with what you actually ordered
Open the accepted purchase record. Identify each option and its ordered quantity, then list the physical components included in each option. The same product photograph can illustrate a full set and separate pieces. If you bought “top only,” a missing pair of trousers is not a warehouse shortage even when the seller's main picture displays a complete outfit.

Consider a stationery order containing two pencil cases and one refill kit. The kit includes three refills according to the paid option. Your purchase therefore has three ordered units and five principal physical pieces. A seller might pack the refills inside one pencil case, place them in a separate sleeve or send them later. Keep the expected physical count alongside the order units so that a warehouse entry stating one kit can be interpreted correctly.

## Ask what the seller dispatched under each number
Find each domestic carrier number associated with the purchase. A seller order can generate more than one waybill if stock is split between locations or dispatched at different times. A replacement label can also create a second number without a second physical parcel. Look for collection and movement events, and ask the seller which numbers were actually used when the record is unclear.

Write a line for each waybill with the carrier, latest event and contents the seller says it contains. Leave the contents unknown if the seller has not confirmed them. A small parcel's weight cannot establish that it contains the refills rather than one empty pencil case. Guessing the allocation may seem efficient until the guesses conflict with the warehouse pictures and send the investigation toward the wrong box.

## Give each stage its own status
CNShopper's tracking guide describes seller dispatch, warehouse arrival, matching to an order, inspection and subsequent international delivery as separate stages. A carrier can report a delivery before you can identify its stocked goods. Conversely, an item in the warehouse can be ready for review while another carton from the same purchase remains in transit. Mark those states independently.

For the stationery example, suppose waybill A shows delivery and a warehouse image clearly shows one pencil case. Waybill B shows collection without delivery. You can say one case is visually accounted for and another domestic parcel is moving. You cannot yet conclude that one case and a refill kit were lost. The next useful question is what seller B's carton contains and whether the first case has any smaller pieces packed inside.

## Inspect the right entry without overinterpreting a photograph
Match the warehouse reference, option label, colour and count with the expected pieces. Inspect the entire photo group when available. A photograph of a closed container establishes little about accessories inside. A picture showing an open pencil case and wrapped refills can support a count if the pieces are visible enough to distinguish. Describe exactly what the image confirms.

If the critical part is hidden, request a targeted check subject to available services. “For the navy pencil case on entry X, please check whether the separately ordered refill kit is inside the zipped pocket” is specific. A request for a new front view of the closed case will probably repeat information you already have. State whether opening sealed retail packaging is acceptable before assuming staff can expose every part.

## Distinguish three common discrepancies
The first is a timing difference: one of several known parcels has not arrived. Continue tracking that specific carrier number. The second is an identity difference: the parcel arrived but the warehouse description or reference is hard to match. Provide the seller and carrier evidence to CNShopper. The third is a contents difference: the relevant carton is identified, but a purchased component is absent from the recorded contents. Ask for a contents check and compare it with the merchant's packing statement.

These outcomes can coexist. The first box might contain the correct pencil case but the wrong colour, while the second is still in transit. Record the visible colour problem separately from the missing-arrival question. Solving one will not solve the other. If you report the whole order as simply incomplete, support may answer the carrier question and miss the fact that a visible item could require action before dispatch.

## Decide whether partial dispatch serves your purpose
After identifying the arrived units, decide whether you need them together. Two independent gifts can be useful on different timelines. A case ordered specifically to hold the missing tools may be unsuitable to send without them. Consider any relevant seller remedy, warehouse storage conditions, packing requirements and actual parcel options before selecting the available entries for international delivery.

Compare the full alternatives. One quote for the current pieces does not represent the cost of shipping the later pieces separately. A future combined quote may also change when all dimensions are known. Record the chosen scenario as “dispatch confirmed item now; remaining items held for intake” or “wait for all matched pieces.” That wording prevents a later reader from assuming every item on the original purchase was inside the first outbound parcel.

## Assemble a small exception message
Start with the order reference and a one-sentence description of what was purchased. Add the relevant carrier number, current delivery event, warehouse entry and the exact unit still in question. Explain which units you have already matched. Attach the order confirmation and the image or merchant response that demonstrates the discrepancy. More screenshots are helpful only if each contributes a different fact.

An example is: “The order was for two cases and one three-piece refill kit. One case is visible under entry X. The seller says the kit travelled under waybill A with that case, while the second case is on waybill B. Can you check whether the refill pouch was recorded inside entry X?” Add the actual references privately. This message asks the warehouse to verify a specific claim without asking it to explain a second carton that has not arrived.

## Close the purchase by unit, then by parcel
When another warehouse entry appears, compare it with the outstanding list rather than restarting the investigation. If the second case arrives and its pocket contains the refills, update both the physical count and the seller's earlier packing assumption. Keep the correction dated. If the refills remain absent and all domestic cartons have been checked, you now have a more precise basis for an after-sales inquiry.

Finish by mapping every accepted unit to a warehouse reference and, later, to its outbound parcel. A product can be ordered, received, inspected and shipped on four different dates. Keeping those dates and counts separate allows you to say exactly where something is missing. It also keeps a normal split delivery from turning into an unnecessary claim while preserving the evidence you need if a shortage is real.

When you release the first items for shipping, save the exact list you selected. Later, the account may show a delivered international parcel alongside a newly arrived warehouse item from the same original purchase. A saved selection lets you recognize this as the outcome you chose. It also prevents you from reporting the later item as missing from a parcel that was never supposed to contain it.
