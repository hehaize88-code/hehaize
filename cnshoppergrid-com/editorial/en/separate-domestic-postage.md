# CNShopper order costs: separating seller postage from international freight
A shipping amount beside a Chinese marketplace product and a shipping amount displayed for an outbound parcel can both be correct. They cover different journeys. The first can pay the seller to get goods to the warehouse. The second can pay for movement from the warehouse toward your destination. Mixing them in one spreadsheet column makes a budget hard to check, especially when several purchases share a parcel.

## Follow the goods rather than the word shipping
Draw the route in two parts. The merchant sends one or more units to the warehouse. Once goods are available and you have selected items for a parcel, the international delivery begins. CNShopper's ordering guide describes confirming product cost and domestic delivery before purchase, then reviewing the warehouse items and choosing international shipping. Use those distinct events as the basis of your purchasing record.

This does not imply that every seller charges domestic postage or that every checkout item has the same treatment. A seller may show delivery included, charge once for the order or change the amount according to the actual selection. Record precisely what the paid order shows. An empty shipping field on a catalogue card is an unknown input until you have verified how the live purchase handles it.

## Use the seller order as the domestic accounting unit
Create one line for each seller order with the chosen goods subtotal, any domestic delivery charge and the transaction currency. If you want item-level detail, put the pieces beneath that seller order. Avoid automatically attaching the full domestic charge to each piece. Doing so counts the same movement several times.

Imagine seller A supplies two folders at 30 each and charges 8 to send both to the warehouse. The seller-order subtotal is 60 and the combined domestic cost is 68. Recording each folder as 30 plus 8 gives a total of 76 and overstates what was spent. You can allocate 4 of domestic cost to each folder for your internal analysis, but the seller charged 8 once. Keep the original invoice total alongside any optional allocation.

## Add a second seller without changing the first invoice
Now suppose seller B supplies one binder at 50 plus 6 domestic delivery. The second purchase costs 56. Your combined purchase-stage cost for the three pieces is 124 in this illustrative example: 68 from seller A and 56 from seller B. Nothing about that sum identifies the price of the eventual international parcel. It is simply the cost of obtaining these items at the warehouse under the stated assumptions.

This separation becomes important when you later decide which goods should travel together. If you ship the binder first and the folders later, the original seller invoices stay the same. Your outbound parcel costs now belong to two parcel records. If all three pieces travel together, there is one outbound record to link to both seller purchases. The seller-to-warehouse history should not need to be rewritten each time you change a consolidation plan.

## Keep estimates out of the paid column
At the planning stage you may want a freight allowance even before you have packed dimensions. Put that figure in an estimate field with its assumptions. Mark whether the input weight represents the item, retail packaging or proposed outbound box. Record the destination, route and date checked. When the packed quote arrives, retain the original assumption as a comparison rather than adding both figures as actual expenditure.

For example, your worksheet might contain an international allowance of 70 and later show an accepted quote of 82 in the same currency. Your freight budget moved by 12. You did not spend 152 merely because both figures appear in the file. If a separate optional service was purchased, give it its own field and determine whether the quote already includes it before adding it again.

## Separate currency conversion from delivery changes
Keep the seller currency visible even when you prefer a home-currency budget. A catalogue conversion, the amount at purchase and the payment provider's posted charge may differ because they were calculated at different times or under different conversion rules. Compare source amounts in a common currency before attributing a difference to postage.

Consider an order where the seller invoice stays at 68 in the original currency but the converted estimate on a later visit changes. The domestic delivery line did not necessarily move. Check the original goods and postage lines first. If those are unchanged, review conversion and any distinct payment-provider charge separately. For a settled purchase, preserve the actual payment record rather than continuously recalculating what you historically paid from today's rate.

## Allocate outbound freight only when it serves a decision
You may want to estimate the delivered cost of individual products. Decide which allocation rule matches your purpose and label it as your analysis. Equal division is easy when the pieces are similar. Dividing by item weight can be more informative for a dense binder and two light folders. Neither method automatically represents the carrier's true price per item; carriers typically quote the parcel as a whole under its service conditions.

Suppose the outbound example costs 90 and you allocate it equally across three pieces. Each receives an internal allocation of 30. If one rigid binder forces the box to be much larger, an equal split hides that fact. To investigate whether the binder should travel separately, compare real parcel scenarios with and without it. Arithmetic allocation explains how you divided an existing expense; a controlled quote comparison informs the separate decision about how to ship.

## Handle changes, refunds and split fulfilment explicitly
If a merchant changes the quantity or issues a partial refund, update the seller purchase record with an adjustment. Keep the original payment and the adjustment as separate events. A return of one item does not automatically remove the entire domestic delivery charge from the original transaction. Check the actual amounts credited and any remaining service entries rather than reversing a charge because the goods count changed.

For a split seller shipment, keep the link between one seller invoice and multiple domestic carrier numbers. The existence of two labels does not prove that two domestic charges were paid by you. Conversely, two purchases from the same merchant need not share one delivery charge. Use the actual checkout and confirmed adjustments as your evidence. A transport event and a financial event are related, but their counts need not match.

## Reconcile the budget when the parcel is ready
At dispatch, start with confirmed seller-order totals, then add the outbound freight and separate services once. Check the currency for each component and note which values are still estimates. If two figures disagree, ask which record they describe before asking why the amount changed. A mismatch frequently becomes understandable when you discover that one figure describes a single merchant and the other describes a mixed parcel.

A useful final note reads like a short explanation: “Two seller orders paid, three pieces received, one international parcel selected. Domestic delivery counted once on each invoice; packing and freight recorded against the parcel.” This gives you enough structure to investigate a price change without assigning it to the wrong party and to compare future purchases without forgetting the second journey.

A useful last check is to add the domestic charges at the same level at which they were quoted. If three lines share one seller shipment and the seller gave one combined figure, enter it once in the purchase total. If the account ultimately records separate domestic entries, reconcile those against the combined quote. Save the seller's explanation if different fulfillment locations or separate waybills changed how the goods were sent.
