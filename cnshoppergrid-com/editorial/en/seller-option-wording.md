# Keeping the seller's option wording intact in a CNShopper order
Two options can look identical after translation even though the seller treats them as different products. A code, suffix or small drawing in the selector can distinguish size systems, bundle contents, revisions or finishes. Preserve enough of the original selection that a buyer can reproduce your choice. A readable English description helps explain your intention; the seller's exact wording helps identify the control that actually orders it.

## Capture the selection rather than the product title
A product title generally describes the family of items. The option identifies which member of that family you want. Open the seller page, make the complete selection and save the exact wording beside the product link. If the option is a picture with little text, save a screenshot showing which swatch is highlighted and how it relates to the product.

Consider a notebook listing titled “A5 travel journal, multiple colours.” The title supplies almost no information about whether the selected pages are lined, dotted or blank. A thumbnail displaying a closed navy cover can represent all three. If the selector specifies a code such as B2, preserve that code exactly along with your reading of it. Do not assume B2 means blank unless the seller's own option key establishes the connection.

## Keep the original and your interpretation in separate fields
When you translate a seller option, keep the source phrase visible beside your translation. A purchaser can then check whether your plain-language interpretation matches the actual button. This also allows you to correct an interpretation later without losing the only reliable record of the selection. Copy significant punctuation, model suffixes and units exactly where practicable.

If a translated label reads “large” while the source option ends in a numeric code, compare that code with the seller's size chart. The code could indicate a garment size, model year, capacity or pack count. Other sellers may use the same English word differently. Do not silently replace a code with an assumed meaning; state the exact selection and flag the attribute that still needs confirmation.

## Separate the things you select together
Describe the independent selector dimensions. A shirt listing may use one row for colour, another for size and another for fit. A desk product might have separate selectors for width, finish and whether mounting hardware is included. Copying a single combined colour name without the other controls leaves an incomplete specification.

An example note for a shelf might say, “Selected finish shown as ash grey in the attached source text; width option 60 cm; mounting kit included; quantity one.” If the seller offers 60 centimetres as the inner usable width or as the total outside width, identify which one you need and request confirmation. The note should describe both what you clicked and what remains unknown. A technically precise number is of little use if it represents the wrong measurement.

## Recheck the option after changing another selector
Some combinations depend on an earlier choice. Changing the finish may reset a size, replace the lead image or hide a bundle. After choosing the last field, reread all active options from the final summary. Save the screenshot at that point. A picture captured two screens earlier may show a combination that is no longer selected.

Suppose you initially choose a white double-layer pencil case and then switch to navy. The seller offers navy only in the single-layer version. If the interface resets the second selector, your final choice may be navy single-layer even though you remember tapping double-layer a minute earlier. Compare the displayed subtotal and option summary with the written requirements. If the exact combination cannot be reproduced, ask the seller what is available before submitting a manual request.

## Distinguish similar labels with a comparison pair
When two options look alike, capture them side by side in your notes with their differences. One might show “with strap,” another “replacement strap.” A mistranslation that reduces both to “strap” could turn a bag purchase into an accessory purchase. Compare the unit, price difference, included pieces and seller option description before interpreting the intended selection.

For a real order, write down the rejected alternative as well as the chosen option if confusion is likely. A useful sentence is, “I want the complete green bag with shoulder strap. Do not select the spare shoulder strap offered under the same listing.” That instruction names the actual risk. A vague phrase such as “choose the correct version” leaves the purchaser with exactly the ambiguity you were trying to remove.

## Match the confirmed order back to the source evidence
CNShopper's ordering guide asks buyers to provide size, colour, quantity and relevant seller notes before purchase. When a confirmation is available, compare its final option with the saved seller selection. Do this while a discrepancy can still be queried. If the confirmation abbreviates a long option, ask for clarification about the missing suffix or included component rather than accepting a generic matching title.

If a seller relists the same product under a new item number, treat the new page as a new offer. Keep the original record and compare both. Does the same option name still describe the same dimensions and pieces? Are the photographs reused from an earlier version? A note pasted from the old listing is a starting point for comparison. It does not establish that the new seller menu maps codes in the same way.

## Use warehouse photos to test the requirement
At intake, ask which part of the option can actually be checked visually. Colour, printed size, visible construction and included pieces may be observable; material composition or long-term durability usually needs different evidence. A photograph of a closed box can show an option label without proving that every advertised component is inside. Frame your question around what a warehouse check can reasonably establish.

For the pencil case example, a straight-on exterior photograph might show navy but leave the number of layers unclear. Request a view of the open compartments tied to the correct warehouse entry, subject to the available service. Compare it against the confirmed order and your saved source selection. If the received unit is the single-layer option, the original wording and screenshot provide a much stronger explanation than a later claim that you remember selecting something else.

## Keep an orderly record when the seller answers
If you receive clarification, save the seller's reply with its date and the specific listing. Record whether it confirms the existing option or proposes a substitute. For example, confirmation that “code C includes the mounting kit” is different from an offer to add the kit separately for an additional amount. Update the written specification if the proposed change is accepted.

Once the transaction is complete, retain the original option, the accepted summary and the evidence you used to approve the warehouse item. When you revisit the spreadsheet months later, you may see a different option order or different translations. Your record will still show what the seller offered at the time and what you actually commissioned. That is the practical value of keeping the seller's wording: it survives changes to the presentation long enough to answer questions about your particular purchase.

For matching pieces, check option consistency across more than one listing. A case and its separate strap can both be described as green while carrying different supplier color names or attachment widths. Ask for the precise label or measurement that connects the two. If a matching finish is essential, make that condition explicit before paying for either part. Two labels that translate into the same English word can still refer to separate finishes within the seller's own catalogue.
