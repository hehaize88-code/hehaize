# CNShopper spreadsheet search: when to use a product link and when a photo helps
If you have the seller's original product link, start there. If you have only a picture, image search can lead you to candidate listings. The evidence required to identify the original changes with the starting point. A product identifier can link two records on the same marketplace. A visually similar image helps narrow a search, but it cannot tell you which seller posted a photograph first or which option a buyer selected.

## Decide what you are trying to recover
There are two useful search goals. You may want the very same seller listing because you need a compatible replacement piece or want to check a previous order. Alternatively, you may simply want a product with a similar appearance. Write down which goal applies. A result that is excellent for visual inspiration may be insufficient for replacing a specific component.

Suppose your picture shows a transparent desk drawer with a distinctive curved handle. If you just want a similar organiser, several matching shapes may be worth comparing. If you need another drawer to fit an existing frame, exact external width, rail spacing and depth are decisive. In the second case, the photograph is the beginning of identification rather than enough evidence to place the order.

## Use a source link when one exists
Open the marketplace link and record the seller, item identifier and option text. Check whether the page still offers the relevant combination. CNShopper's ordering guide describes submitting a product link with size, colour, quantity and other purchase details. A saved link makes a strong starting point, yet it can still point to a listing whose available variants have changed since you first saw it.

If your saved address opens a share landing page, navigate to the product and compare its final identity. If it opens a shop, search result or expired offer, do not treat the generic page as the original product. Look for the identifier in the original receipt, seller conversation or page title if available. Keep the marketplace with the number; identical-looking numbers drawn from different services are not automatically comparable.

## Prepare a photo that contains useful evidence
Crop a search image to one main object while retaining enough of its shape to distinguish it. A close crop of an ordinary metal zip can return many unrelated products. A wider view showing the zip position, unusual pocket and overall outline gives the search more relevant features. Save the uncropped original because it may contain details needed later even if they distract from the initial search.

Try another angle when the first image is dominated by a generic printed graphic or background. For the drawer, a front view may show the curved handle while a side view reveals the rail profile. Those two views answer different questions. Keep track of which image produced which candidate rather than assuming that a similar first result under both searches proves a common source.

## Compare structure before colour
Look for features that remain recognizable under different lighting: screw positions, number of pockets, spacing of openings, seam direction, underside features or the shape of a hinge. Colour alone can vary with white balance, editing and screen settings. Use visible differences to rule out candidates first; one incompatible structural detail may matter more than five generic similarities.

Imagine two transparent drawers shown at different angles. Both have a curved handle and the same general outline. One uses a single central rail, while the other has two side rails. If your existing frame requires the twin-rail version, the single-rail candidate is unsuitable regardless of how close its colour appears. Mark that reason in your shortlist. It prevents the same appealing but incompatible result from re-entering the decision during a later search.

## Treat reused photographs as weak evidence of identity
Multiple sellers can display the same promotional photograph. The fact that the images match exactly may say more about the image source than the physical stock. Open each candidate listing and compare the seller identity, available variants, dimensions, package contents and current order terms. If a product has seller-specific or warehouse photographs, use them to examine the details the promotional view leaves uncertain.

Avoid claiming an item is genuine, identical in material or made by a particular manufacturer because two images match. Image search compares visible appearance. It cannot establish the underlying supply chain, a product's functional performance or which unit the merchant will dispatch. If those facts matter, seek evidence appropriate to the question and choose whether the remaining uncertainty is acceptable.

## Confirm the selected variant independently
A correct listing can still produce the wrong item. Once you have identified a candidate, select the exact size, colour and package option and see whether the image or price changes. Save the original option wording with your notes. An organiser listing might combine a frame, a spare drawer and a complete unit. The picture showing a finished organiser does not establish that all three options include the frame.

For a replacement purchase, write a compatibility specification. Record the measurements and connection details from your existing item using named endpoints. Compare those with the actual seller information. If the seller measures the inside width while you need the outside width at the rails, the numbers are not directly comparable. Ask the seller or agent to clarify the specific dimension instead of treating a visually convincing match as proof that the part will fit.

## Build a shortlist that shows what remains unknown
Use a small table or note with four fields: candidate source link, features that match, features that contradict and facts still needed. A candidate with no visible contradiction may still lack the one measurement that controls fit. Keeping unknowns separate from matches makes the decision clearer. It also helps you phrase a question that someone can answer without reviewing your entire search history.

For example, candidate A may match the handle and two rails but lack a width measurement. Candidate B may have a confirmed width and an incompatible single rail. Candidate C may use the exact promotional picture but offer no usable option details. The next useful action is to ask about candidate A's external width. Searching for another dozen indistinguishable front photographs is unlikely to resolve that particular uncertainty.

## Connect the eventual order to the evidence
When you select a listing, save the actual seller page, chosen option, required measurement and purchase reference. At warehouse intake, check the feature that made the candidate acceptable. If fit depended on twin rails, a front picture alone cannot confirm them. Ask what inspection options are available and request a view showing the relevant location, keeping the image associated with the correct warehouse entry.

Finally, record whether you recovered the original listing or selected a visually similar alternative. Both can be sensible outcomes. A replacement part benefits from a stronger identity and measurement chain; an aesthetic shortlist can tolerate more variation. Being explicit about the outcome prevents a helpful search result from becoming an unsupported guarantee about a seller, variant or product that the photograph could never establish.

If your search started with a picture of an item you already own, measure the feature that makes it useful to you. A replacement pouch might need to fit an existing insert, or a cover might need openings in a particular position. Record that dimension or layout before browsing visually similar models. When you narrow the results to two listings, compare how clearly each seller documents that requirement. A slightly less similar photograph can lead to the better-supported choice if its details answer the practical question that motivated the search.
