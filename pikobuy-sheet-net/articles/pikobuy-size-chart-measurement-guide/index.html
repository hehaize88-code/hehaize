PikoBuy Size Chart Guide: Compare Measurements Correctly

A size letter is not a measurement. “Large” can describe different cuts, endpoints and intended bodies across two listings, even when the products look similar. This PikoBuy size chart guide builds a repeatable comparison from an item that already fits, preserves the seller’s evidence and defines what a warehouse measurement can—and cannot—confirm.

Identify what the chart actually measures

Attach every chart to the exact product ID, selected variant and review date. Record whether the values appear to describe the garment itself or a recommended body range. If the chart does not say, mark the basis unknown. Do not choose the interpretation that produces the preferred size.

Copy the units and named measurements exactly. Convert units only in a separate calculated column, retaining the original. Note whether chest means circumference or flat width, whether length begins at the highest shoulder point or collar seam, and whether sleeve length includes the shoulder. A number without an endpoint is incomplete evidence.

Keep seller notes about manual-measurement tolerance as seller-stated context. Do not apply one listing's tolerance to another product. The useful record is chart, method, units, option and date together.

Measure a reference garment, not your memory

Choose a garment that fits the way the new product should fit. Describe its category, material, stretch, cut and how it is worn. Lay it flat without pulling, then record the same endpoints used by the listing. Photograph or diagram the endpoints for repeatability.

A hoodie worn over a T-shirt needs a different reference from a fitted jersey. Trousers require waist method, rise, inseam and leg opening rather than a generic size conversion. Shoes require foot length and the listing's specific size basis; an EU label alone does not establish internal length.

Measure twice and record the date. If two readings differ, improve the method rather than averaging immediately. The goal is a stable comparison, not a precise-looking number.

Build a measurement matching matrix

Create one row for each comparable endpoint. Columns should include reference value, seller value, difference, stated tolerance, stretch or construction note and decision impact. Leave the comparison blank when endpoints do not match. A missing number is safer than a false equivalence.

Define acceptable ranges from the intended fit. For a relaxed top, chest ease may matter more than a small length difference. For trousers, rise and thigh can dominate the waist label. Do not let a spreadsheet total hide a critical must-have measurement.

Use pass, question or fail for each endpoint. Then write a short reason. “Question: seller chest is circumference but reference is flat width” points to an action; “medium fit” does not.

Ask one question that resolves the largest uncertainty

When a chart is ambiguous, ask for the measurement method tied to the selected size. Name the product, size, endpoint and units. Avoid sending a broad “What size should I buy?” question without body, fit or reference information. A seller recommendation is evidence of the seller's advice, not a guarantee of fit.

Save the answer with date and source. If the seller sends a new chart, preserve the earlier version rather than overwriting it. A later change may explain a mismatch after arrival.

PikoBuy's guide tells buyers to confirm specification, colour and size before order submission. Use the imported option screen as a final checkpoint: selected size text, colour, quantity and price state must match the spreadsheet row.

Request warehouse measurements with named endpoints

Plan measurements only where the result can change the keep decision. State the garment position, endpoint, units and whether the item should be relaxed. For a top: “flat chest from underarm seam to underarm seam, straight across.” For trousers: specify waistband method and whether it is relaxed or stretched.

PikoBuy's public materials describe warehouse photo confirmation and allow buyers to review inspection photos; additional detailed photos may be available. A visible tape can support a measurement observation, but camera angle, fabric movement and endpoint placement introduce uncertainty. Use a reasonable acceptance range rather than demanding impossible precision.

Keep seller chart, reference garment and warehouse reading in separate columns. The comparison, not the replacement of one with another, is the value.

Know what a size workflow cannot promise

Measurements do not fully predict comfort. Fabric stretch, thickness, drape, construction, posture and personal preference still matter. Photos cannot prove fibre composition or how a garment behaves after washing. A matching chest value can coexist with narrow shoulders or an unsuitable armhole.

Do not publish universal height-and-weight recommendations as facts unless a seller provides them for the exact item, and even then label them as seller guidance. Do not turn one successful order into a permanent rule for another seller or batch.

The honest output is a confidence level and remaining uncertainty. High confidence means matching endpoints and adequate range, not guaranteed fit.

Use a fit decision record

  1. Attach the exact chart to the exact product and variant.
  2. Identify garment versus body basis.
  3. Normalize units without deleting originals.
  4. Measure a relevant reference garment.
  5. Compare only matching endpoints.
  6. Ask one precise seller question where needed.
  7. Request warehouse evidence only when decision-relevant.
  8. Record the final choice and accepted uncertainty.

Mark the row order-ready when must-have endpoints pass and remaining unknowns are acceptable. Keep it in research when one chart clarification could resolve the choice. Reject it when a critical endpoint is missing, incompatible or outside the defined range.

A PikoBuy size chart becomes useful when it is part of a method. The method survives different products because it compares evidence, not letters.

Change the method for the product category

Tops usually need chest, length, shoulder and sleeve endpoints. Trousers may require relaxed and stretched waist, rise, thigh, inseam and opening. Bags need exterior dimensions and usable opening; shoes need foot-length basis, selected label and, only when available, an internal-length method. Do not force every category into the same columns.

For stretchy garments, record whether the chart value is relaxed and whether the seller states a stretch range. For structured clothing, shoulder and armhole construction can matter even when chest width appears suitable. For sets, attach each measurement to the correct piece.

The shared rule is not a universal field list. It is evidence attached to an exact product, consistent endpoints, a relevant reference and a carefully stated acceptance range.
