# Comparing estimated and packed dimensions in Superbuy's shipping calculator
An estimate based on a seller's product dimensions and a quote based on a closed outbound carton can produce different results even when nothing has gone wrong. The first figure may describe a bare item. The second includes everything that occupies space in transit. Before comparing a Superbuy shipping estimate with a parcel record, identify what each number measures and bring both scenarios onto the same basis.

## Collect three measurements, their units and their object
Record length, width and height together. A single “size” such as 40 centimetres cannot tell you whether a box is long and narrow or nearly square. Identify the object measured: loose product, retail box, seller delivery carton or packed outbound parcel. A measurement is useful only when you know which of these four things it describes.

Take a pair of shoes as an example. The foot length on a size chart is a fit measurement. The outsole length describes a shoe. The shoebox dimensions describe a retail package. The external shipping carton may contain the shoebox plus padding and another item. Entering the foot length as the longest side of the parcel because it is the only visible number creates a precise-looking estimate from an unrelated measurement.

## Normalize units before comparing anything
Superbuy's shipping calculator displays weight in grams and dimensions in centimetres. Read the labels on the version you are actually using. Convert a weight given in kilograms by multiplying by one thousand. Convert millimetres to centimetres by dividing by ten. Keep the original numbers in your notes as well as the converted values; this makes it easier to identify a transcription error later.

Suppose a seller lists a box as 420 by 260 by 150 millimetres and the scale reads 1.80 kilograms. Its corresponding inputs are 42 by 26 by 15 centimetres and 1,800 grams. If one side is accidentally entered as 150 centimetres, the box in the calculator becomes ten times taller than the box on the desk. A disappearing route or implausibly high figure can be a reason to recheck units before assuming the carrier has changed its prices.

## Name the assumptions in the first estimate
Record the destination, warehouse, category and planned contents alongside the estimated weight and dimensions. These fields can affect which results are displayed. Two estimates with identical dimensions but different destinations are not a controlled test of packaging. Likewise, a different item category may change eligibility even if the physical package stays the same.

Write a short scenario summary such as “one pair, original shoebox kept, outer carton dimensions unknown, using seller box dimensions as a lower-information estimate.” This tells you exactly what you will need to replace when the warehouse provides measurements. Avoid describing the provisional result as your confirmed freight. You may have an accurate seller measurement while still missing the relevant shipping measurement.

## Calculate the space difference without inventing a freight rule
For a rectangular box, multiply length by width by height to compare volumes. If an illustrative first estimate uses 40 by 25 by 15 centimetres, the rectangular volume is 15,000 cubic centimetres. A measured outer package of 42 by 28 by 18 is 21,168 cubic centimetres. The difference is 6,168 cubic centimetres, around 41 percent more space than the estimate.

That comparison describes geometry only. Whether a particular carrier uses dimensional billing, how it converts volume into a billing weight, how it rounds and how much it charges are separate questions. Read the selected route rules instead of borrowing a divisor from a different carrier's tutorial. A 41 percent increase in volume does not prove that freight increased by 41 percent. The actual weight, route limits and rate bands may matter more.

## Measure the longest point that actually travels
Use the maximum outside dimensions of the packed item or carton as instructed by the carrier. Corner protectors, end caps and bulging sides can extend past an otherwise tidy rectangle. Ask which dimensions were returned by the warehouse if the values appear to describe only the item inside. Record measurements in a consistent order and label the longest side explicitly when route limits make it important.

A rolled print is a useful illustration. The artwork size is a flat rectangle. Its tube has a length and diameter. If several tubes are bundled in a rectangular carton, the outbound dimensions are different again. Comparing the flat sheet's height with the bundle's longest side tells you very little. Write down which packing state each entry represents before deciding that one of the measurements must be wrong.

## Change one variable at a time
To investigate a quote difference, start from the closest reproducible version of the first estimate. Substitute the packed dimensions while keeping destination, warehouse, item category, weight and route selection constant if possible. Record the new result. Then substitute the packed weight and compare again. If an input makes the original route unavailable, note that as an eligibility change rather than comparing two different services as though they were the same line.

Consider an item weighing 1,800 grams in its seller packaging. Its final packed weight becomes 2,100 grams. If you change the box size, weight and destination at the same time, the resulting difference cannot tell you which factor mattered. A pair of controlled estimates identifies whether one change appears sufficient to alter the quote. The calculator remains an estimate; the exercise is designed to clarify assumptions rather than reproduce a carrier invoice exactly.

## Reconcile the deposit separately from optional services
Superbuy's guidance distinguishes the international shipping deposit from final shipping based on verified measurements and the selected service. When looking at a total, separate the freight component from selected packing, other services and any distinct adjustments displayed in the account. Preserve the original currency and keep the actual parcel reference with every amount.

If your card statement is in a different currency, first compare the two Superbuy records in their common transaction currency. Only then investigate any payment conversion. Otherwise a difference in exchange rates can look like a change in packed weight. A useful support question specifies the original estimate, packed measurements, route, deposit and the single unmatched amount. “My shipping changed” leaves too many possible explanations unresolved.

## Keep the final comparison useful for the next parcel
Save the accepted packed dimensions, contents, packing treatment and weight after dispatch. This becomes a reference case for a similar future decision, provided you retain its conditions. Two identical retail items may still yield different outer cartons when one is shipped alone and the other is combined with a book or wrapped with additional protection.

Review what your first estimate got right and what changed. Perhaps the scale weight was close but the protective carton was larger. Next time you know which missing measurement deserves attention before you choose a route. The goal is not to predict a shipping price from memory. It is to recognize which assumptions are provisional, replace them with the measured values and understand the resulting choice before committing the parcel to international delivery.

If the account and your worksheet still disagree, include the original units in your inquiry. A support agent can check a photo of the scale and measuring tape more readily than a number stripped of its unit and packing context.

For a two-box comparison, calculate each rectangular volume separately before adding them. Multiplying the sum of the lengths by the sum of the widths and heights produces an imaginary oversized box. Only use the measured dimensions of a single combined carton if the warehouse has actually combined the goods that way. Keep each individual carton measurement beside its corresponding quote.
