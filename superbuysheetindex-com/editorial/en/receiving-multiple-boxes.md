# Receiving a multi-box purchase at Superbuy without losing track of its pieces
A single purchase can generate two exceptions that look like opposites. One warehouse entry looks incomplete because a second box is still travelling. A second entry looks like a duplicate because the product name matches the first. Both can describe a correctly split purchase. To work out what arrived, count purchases, cartons and individual pieces separately.

## Define the unit the seller agreed to supply
Start with the paid option rather than the lead photograph. A listing showing a desk and chair might sell a desk only, a chair only or a package containing both. The unit on the receipt determines what you should expect to account for. Write it in plain language. If the seller calls a set “one unit,” list the pieces you expect inside it without turning those pieces into additional purchased units.

Suppose you ordered two identical table lamps. The merchant confirms that each lamp comprises a base, shade and fittings packet. Your purchase record therefore describes two units and six expected components. It still tells you nothing about how many boxes the merchant will use. The seller might send each complete lamp separately, group the bases together or place both sets in one outer carton. You need a packing statement to connect those counts.

## Ask for the box map before comparing photos
Ask the seller how many parcels were actually handed over and which carrier number belongs to each. If the merchant supplied a packing list, copy its wording. When contents by box are unknown, say so in your notes. An honest blank tells you what remains to be established. A guessed assignment may silently convert a normal arrival into a false missing-item report.

Give boxes short names in your own notes while keeping the complete carrier number. For the lamps, “box ending 482, expected both bases” is more useful than “large box.” A repacked carton can look different from the seller's photograph, and dimensions can be reported in different orientations. If the waybills came from different carriers, include each carrier's name. Two similar strings can have different meanings in different tracking systems.

## Compare deliveries one by one
Read the status for every waybill independently. If two boxes left together, they can still receive scans at different times. A single order-level status on a marketplace may summarize part of the purchase. Open the underlying delivery details and note the latest event for each actual box. Keep a cancelled label separate from a waybill associated with a collected parcel.

In the lamp example, the box expected to hold the two bases has a delivery scan, while the box containing shades and fittings is still in transit. A warehouse record with two bases is consistent with those facts. Mark the bases as accounted for and the other four components as pending. Do not report two missing lamps simply because the original order quantity was two. The meaningful outstanding question is whether the second carton has been delivered and identified.

## Connect warehouse entries to physical contents
Once goods are stocked, Superbuy's guides direct users to My Warehouse. Inspect item names and photographs alongside your box map. Decide what each picture actually shows. A closed retail carton labelled “shade set” can support identification of a carton without establishing the count or condition of its contents. A visible pair of shades does not establish that both fittings packets are present.

If the arrangement is ambiguous, ask for an inventory check that someone can carry out. An example request is: “For the warehouse entry linked to the domestic waybill ending 482, please confirm whether it contains two lamp bases and whether any fittings packets were recorded with them.” Include the full reference in the private inquiry. Keep the question about contents separate from a second question about damaged finish or measurements. Each has a different piece of evidence that can settle it.

## Handle an unexpected third carton
A merchant may send a small accessory separately without updating the initial packing summary. If a later tracking notice arrives, do not alter the older record to make it appear that you always expected three boxes. Add a dated update naming the source of the new information. Identify which component moved from a previous expectation into the additional carton.

Suppose the merchant reports that the fittings packets were sent by a different carrier. The original second carton is now expected to contain shades only. If the warehouse confirms two shades, that carton can be marked complete even though the overall purchase is still incomplete. Record the third waybill and its expected two fittings packets. This simple distinction matters if the first two cartons are ready to dispatch and the third is delayed.

## Decide whether to wait or ship part of the purchase
An availability decision and a packing decision are separate. You might know exactly where every unit is but still need to decide whether partial international delivery serves your purpose. For a modular lamp, shipping the bases without fittings can leave you with two unusable pieces at home. For several independent notebooks, an early partial shipment may be more practical. Describe the consequence of dispatching the available pieces before comparing prices.

Check the applicable storage position and live parcel options for the actual entries. Compare like assumptions. A partial shipment today and a later shipment of accessories may incur two separate outbound charges. A combined parcel later may have different dimensions, packaging needs and route eligibility. Treat each price as a quote for a named set of contents. Nothing about a domestic seller's choice of box count guarantees which outbound packing arrangement will suit you.

## Prepare an exception record the warehouse can investigate
If the carrier shows all boxes delivered but the account reflects only some of the contents, build a compact exception report. State the merchant order, each carrier and waybill, the expected contents of each carton, and the warehouse entries found. Then name the single unmatched box or missing component. Distinguish the seller's statement from the warehouse photo; being clear about the evidence makes it easier to decide whether the next check belongs with the seller or the receiving team.

Avoid sending ten loosely related screenshots. Select the order confirmation establishing quantity, the dispatch statement establishing box allocation, the specific carrier event and a warehouse image showing the discrepancy. If a screenshot covers other orders or personal information, limit what you share to what the official support channel needs. Retain the originals privately in case a clearer view is requested.

## Reconcile the set when dispatching or unpacking
When all expected pieces are accounted for, write a closure note naming the contents accepted. For the lamps it might read, “Two bases, two shades and two fittings packets checked against the seller confirmation; warehouse entries A, B and C belong to this purchase.” Keep any visible defect or disputed measurement as its own open issue until resolved. A complete component count alone does not settle condition.

If you later combine these goods into one international parcel, attach the outbound reference to that group of warehouse entries. When the shipment reaches you, unpack against the component list rather than trying to remember the merchant's domestic carton arrangement. The seller might have sent three boxes and the international packer might use one. Your original list of required pieces remains useful through either packing choice, and it gives you a precise starting point if something is absent at delivery.

Check loose hardware against the assembly instructions when those instructions are available. A photograph of several small metal pieces rarely establishes which fasteners belong to which unit. Ask the merchant for the expected count by type and keep that statement with the parts list rather than relying on the total weight of the hardware bag.
